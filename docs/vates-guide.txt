1 Preamble
This guide provides foundational insights and strategies for designing robust security within
a virtualized Vates VMS environment. It encompasses a broad range of best practices,
specifically tailored to enhance protection across multiple components. Key areas of fo-
cus include:
• Secure Installation and Deployment of XCP-ng: Guidelines for implementing XCP-ng
with an emphasis on security from the outset.
• Protecting XCP-ng Networks and Storage: Essential measures to safeguard your net-
work and storage from potential threats.
• Configuring Virtual Machines: Tips for secure virtual machine setup and management.
• Securing Virtualized Storage: Tactics to enhance the security of your storage in a virtu-
alized setting.
• Secure Deployment of Xen Orchestra Virtual Appliance: Best practices for deploying
Xen Orchestra in a manner that prioritizes security.
• Comprehensive Configuration of the Entire Stack: Strategies for overall system secu-
rity, covering all layers of the infrastructure.
The recommendations and guidance provided in this document are intended to inform and
direct security efforts but are not exhaustive. Detailed step-by-step procedures are omitted
unless necessary for clarity, allowing for flexibility in adaptation to specific organizational
needs.
1.1 Scope
This guide is specifically tailored for users of XCP-ng 8.3 LTS, as well as Xen Orchestra 6
(XOA on the "stable" and "latest" release channels, or installations from the sources track-
ing the master branch). These are the versions that currently receive security updates.
These recommendations are optimized for the stated software versions, ensuring compat-
ibility and effectiveness in enhancing security measures within these environments. Users
should verify the version of their software against this guide to ensure alignment and appli-
cability of the provided best practices.
XCP-ng 8.2 LTS reached its end of life on September 16, 2025: it no longer receives bug
fixes nor security updates. If you are still running it, upgrading to XCP-ng 8.3 LTS is the first
hardening step to take, before applying any other recommendation of this guide.
1.2 Intended Audience
This guide is crafted for professionals who are actively engaged in maintaining and securing
virtual environments using XCP-ng and Xen Orchestra. It is particularly useful for people re-
sponsible for the day-to-day management of XCP-ng and Xen Orchestra systems (Adminis-
trators), professionals involved in designing robust and secure systems architectures (Sys-
tems Architects) and individuals focused on the security aspects of virtual environments

1.4 Security through transparency
The philosophy of "Security through transparency" upheld by the entire Vates stack, which
includes XCP-ng, Xen Orchestra, and other programs, stands in stark contrast to the contro-
versial practice of security through obscurity. Security through obscurity relies on secrecy
and the concealment of design or implementation details as the primary method of pro-
viding security. This approach is often criticized because it presumes that vulnerabilities
in a system remain safe as long as they are not discovered, which is a risky and unreliable
defense.
In contrast, the Vates stack’s open-source model exemplifies the belief that transparency
leads to higher security standards. By opening up the software’s source code and devel-
opment processes to public scrutiny, vulnerabilities are more likely to be identified and
remedied quickly, not just by the original developers but by anyone in the community who
can view and improve the code. This communal vetting process helps fortify the software
against attacks and reduces the likelihood of persistent security flaws.
Moreover, at Vates, transparency extends beyond just the source code. The entire build
process, security workflows, and documentation are also open and accessible. This allows
for collective improvement and ensures that every aspect of our systems is subjected to
rigorous review and continuous enhancement. However, openness does not mean that any-
one can push changes without verification. We maintain a stringent review process where
proposed changes undergo thorough scrutiny by experienced developers and security ex-
perts. This balance of openness and strict oversight ensures that while the community can
contribute to improvements, all modifications are carefully vetted to maintain the highest
security standards.
This open approach ensures that security does not rely on keeping operational details secret
but rather on rigorous review and continuous improvements driven by an active community.
Such transparency not only builds trust among users and developers but also fosters a more
secure environment where security is based on solid architecture and shared knowledge
rather than on concealment. Thus, the Vates stack, including XCP-ng and Xen Orchestra,
promotes a robust security model grounded in openness and collaboration, complemented
by a vigilant and meticulous review process.

1.5 Terminology
Vates VMS: "Vates Virtualization and Management Stack", which is the entire virtualization
stack running XCP-ng and Xen Orchestra.
XCP-ng: The virtualization platform, running the compute, storage and networking part.
Xen Orchestra: The backup and orchestration platform. It’s the name of the software, but
not of the virtual Appliance that is containing more things (see just below).
XOA: Xen Orchestra virtual Appliance. This is the turnkey distribution to use Xen Orchestra
in production and with support. XOA is part of the Vates VMS Stack.
XO 6 and XO 5: The two web interfaces of Xen Orchestra. XO 6 is the new interface, built
on top of the REST API; XO 5 is the historical interface, built on top of the JSON-RPC
API. Both are shipped with Xen Orchestra 6 and manage the same infrastructure.
XO Lite: The lightweight web interface embedded in every XCP-ng 8.3 host, used to man-
age a single pool without deploying Xen Orchestra.
VSA: Vates Security Advisory. The official document describing a security issue affecting
the Vates stack, its impact, the affected versions and how to fix or mitigate it, identified
as VSA-YYYY-NNN.
Guest Network: This term refers to networks that transmit data originating from or di-
rected to virtual machines, often called VM networks. They are essential for handling
the virtual machine traffic within your network.
Management Interface: This interface comprises a NIC (Network Interface Card) or a VLAN
configured on a NIC. It is assigned an IP address used by XCP-ng for managing network
operations, including traffic between hosts, integration with Xen Orchestra Load Bal-
ancer plugin, and live migration processes. This IP address is also what Xen Orchestra
connects to.
Control Domain: Known as Domain 0 or "dom0," this is a specialized VM instance, based on
the Linux kernel, that operates exclusively on each XCP-ng host. It is the primary privi-
leged domain capable of making privileged hypervisor calls, such as mapping physical
memory to and from domains. The control domain has exclusive rights to manage di-
rect access to physical I/O resources and inspect the data within other domains (e.g.,
Domain U).
The Management API: Also known as XAPI, this API is designed for the management of
XCP-ng environments, enabling remote configuration and control of domains across
hosts in a XCP-ng pool. This network is used by Xen Orchestra to do all the manage-
ment and backup operations.

2 Introduction
The security measures you implement within your virtual environment are influenced by
your organization’s tolerance for risk, alongside external considerations such as regulatory
and compliance requirements. The location of your virtual infrastructure—whether it’s in a
co-location facility, a private cloud, or integrated within your corporate network—also sig-
nificantly impacts the security strategies you adopt due to varying risk profiles associated
with each setting. For instance, while a co-location facility might face security threats from
other tenants, it often benefits from robust, continuous security monitoring. Conversely,
internal threats might be less prevalent in enterprise environments, which can lead to a
de-prioritization of securing internal guest networks.
Similarly, many security principles and practices applicable to physical infrastructures are
also relevant to virtual environments. It is crucial for the hypervisor, which isolates and
protects each VM from others, to be fundamentally designed with robust security measures
to ensure safe and reliable operation across the virtual landscape.
2.1 Risk assessment
Improving security through better design and isolation of your virtualized infrastructure is
essential, yet it’s important to recognize that some security solutions may introduce more
constraints than others. Often, a balance must be struck between the ease of use and the
robustness of security measures. We strongly recommend dedicating time to assess the
risks associated with each action within your infrastructure. Understanding these risks is
the first step toward mitigating them effectively. To facilitate this process, various frame-
works are available that can help you systematically evaluate risks. We suggest exploring
the EBIOS Risk Manager framework as a comprehensive tool for conducting these assess-
ments [1]. This approach will enable you to make informed decisions that align with your
security needs and operational requirements.
2.2 Global Strategy
In deploying a complete virtualized environment, it is critical to prioritize the security de-
sign. This necessity arises from the requirement to safeguard both the orchestration, vir-
tualization layer and the virtual machines that operate above it. The complexity of manag-
ing multiple layers, each with distinct life-cycles and administration, increases the security
challenge. Your strategy must address your organization’s security expectations as well as
its governance, risk management, and compliance requirements, alongside those intrinsic
to the VMs within your infrastructure. The diversity in VM types, uses, and governing policies
further complicates security management.
Often, various organizational segments share services and computing resources within many
deployments. Achieving separation among these segments typically requires virtual rather
than physical barriers, underscoring the importance of a security-centric design in virtual-
ization.

To enhance security, it is essential to thoroughly understand and document the following
aspects:
• Traffic and Data Flow: Detailed mapping of traffic between hypervisors, switches, and
guest systems.
• System Components: Comprehensive inventory of all elements in the ecosystem, in-
cluding hypervisors, workloads, networks, management interfaces, and physical hard-
ware.
• Communication Networks: Identification of all communication and data exchanges be-
tween hosts and any other potential communication channels, active or inactive.
• Change Management: Clarity on components that are stable versus those expected to
adapt as different organizational groups evolve.
• ACLs: Assignment of permissions to specific individuals.
• Hardware Management: Inventory of any removable hardware elements, such as USB
drives and removable disk drives.
• Network Configuration Details: Documentation of the management interfaces’ net-
work settings, including IP addresses, physical connections to switches, ports, etc.
2.3 Security alignment
We advise securing guest operating systems in alignment with your organization’s estab-
lished security protocols. Additionally, we suggest securing networks by adhering to the
guidelines set forth in your organization’s security policy. This includes safeguarding net-
work passwords and properly configuring digital certificates to enhance security measures.
Given that the virtualization platform serves as the foundational layer upon which all other
systems and applications operate, it is imperative that it aligns flawlessly with your organi-
zation’s security policies. Ensuring that the virtualization infrastructure upholds and reflects
these policies is crucial for maintaining an overall secure IT environment. This alignment
not only protects the underlying platform but also fortifies the security of the systems and
applications it supports.
2.4 Stack overview
Deploying a virtualized environment requires to understand the main different building blocks:
• The network and storage layer
• The virtualization platform layer, including the hypervisor and its toolstack
• The VM layer, where your applications will run
• The Orchestration layer, managed by Xen Orchestra at scale, potentially between mul-
tiple physical locations
• The Backup layer, also managed by Xen Orchestra, including the Backup Repositories

3 Network and Storage
Network operations within the Vates VMS environment take place on both physical and log-
ical planes. In this setting, XCP-ng functions as a virtual switch, controlling traffic that cir-
culates internally within the host—such as that within private networks—as well as traffic
that flows out to the broader physical network.
This structure underscores the importance of securing each component of the Vates VMS
environment, including the layers of the virtual machine (guest), the hypervisor, and the
physical network, which also involves the storage networks.
To ensure the integrity and security of data, it is critical to enforce a stringent separation
of data across these network layers, preventing any unintended data interchange between
the logical networks within the hypervisor and other external networks.
For a detailed technical overview of networking in XCP-ng, including best practices and con-
figuration tips, refer to the XCP-ng documentation available at https://docs.xcp-ng.
org/networking/. This resource provides comprehensive insights into network setup
and management, essential for optimizing and securing your virtual environment.
3.1 General recommendations
In the pursuit of securing your network infrastructure, it is crucial to implement general best
practices that mitigate the risk of human error and misconfigurations.
A fundamental strategy is ensuring consistency across your pool by equipping each member
with the same number of NICs, which significantly reduces confusion and prevents incorrect
connections in an uncontrolled manner.
Equally important is the use of clear, human-readable names for your networks, applied
consistently across the entire infrastructure to avoid misconfigurations that could compro-
mise network isolation.
Additionally, avoiding the assignment of IP addresses to networks in Dom0 that do not re-
quire them is key to maintaining a streamlined and secure network infrastructure, as un-
necessary complexity often leads to potential security risks.
Furthermore, implementing consistent cabling standards and conventions, such as color
coding and NIC numbering, across your infrastructure plays a critical role in minimizing hu-
man errors.
By standardizing these elements, you create a predictable and orderly network environment
that simplifies maintenance and troubleshooting while reducing the likelihood of security
vulnerabilities due to misconfigurations. These practices form the backbone of a robust
approach to network security, safeguarding your systems from the ground up.

3.2 Network protection
Incorporating physical isolation between networks in XCP-ng environments is a crucial se-
curity measure that serves as a foundational component of a defense-in-depth strategy
[11]. This approach significantly reduces the potential for breaches, transcending the in-
herent security measures of the hypervisor and its network stack. Physical network isola-
tion ensures that even if one segment is compromised, the breach does not automatically
extend to other parts of the infrastructure.
This method of segmentation fundamentally increases the complexity and resource re-
quirements for any potential attacker, making it substantially more challenging to move
laterally across the network. It acts as a robust barrier, reinforcing the security posture by
adding an additional layer of protection that complements the virtualized security controls.
In essence, physical isolation is not just an enhancement; it’s a basic yet powerful tactic
that underpins the overall security architecture, making it markedly more resilient against
attacks.
3.2.1 Management network
During the installation of XCP-ng, each host is configured with a management network,
which is associated with a designated NIC to serve as the management interface. This man-
agement network facilitates essential XCP-ng management activities such as communica-
tion between hosts, live migration, VM import and export, and backups of resource pool
metadata. Importantly, it does not handle traffic between guest virtual machines but does
manage interactions involving Xen Orchestra, which orchestrates the environment.
While XCP-ng allows for the consolidation of management, storage and guest traffic through
a single interface, this setup is generally less secure. Best practices, as detailed in this
guide, advocate for segregating these traffic types to enhance security.
When configuring your environment, it is crucial to ensure that the management network,
especially where Xen Orchestra connects to all nodes, is shielded from access by any un-
secured or public networks. The same applies to Xen Orchestra itself: neither the manage-
ment network nor Xen Orchestra must ever be reachable from the Internet. Administrators
working remotely reach them through a VPN or a Zero Trust access gateway, as detailed in
the section "Never expose Xen Orchestra to the Internet".

3.2.2 Storage network
The network designated for shared storage in XCP-ng, which supports NFS, iSCSI, Fiber
Channel, and similar technologies, is where all VM disk data traverse. It is essential to en-
sure that this network is isolated, private, and secured because of the sensitive nature of
the data being handled. By dedicating a physical network to storage, you not only achieve
a consistent performance level but also gain an additional layer of isolation. Depending on
your security requirements, you might opt for logical isolation, such as using VLANs, for
more convenience at the cost of the isolation level [7].
To enhance the reliability of your storage network (which is critical for the availability of your
VMs), employing network bonding is a standard practice. This adds redundancy, helping to
safeguard against potential failures. Additionally, it is prudent to assign a unique IP sub-
net to the storage network that is segregated and non-routable with other networks, thus
safeguarding against accidental breaches or misconfigurations in the future.
3.2.3 Backup network
Typically, the backup network in most setups defaults to being the same as the manage-
ment network. In this configuration, Xen Orchestra retrieves data from XCP-ng hosts dur-
ing backup operations instead of interacting directly with the storage. This arrangement
allows Xen Orchestra to operate solely on the management network, effectively segregat-
ing it from the storage network without impacting backup processes. However, in scenarios
where backups involve terabytes of data, it’s critical to ensure the management network is
adequately scaled to handle such volumes.

3.2.4 VM network
In the architecture of virtual environments, it is imperative to restrict VM access to critical
operational networks such as management, storage, or backup networks. Typically, VMs are
exposed to the Internet or other untrusted networks to deliver services, making it essential
to consider them as untrusted entities during both the design and exploitation phases, in
line with Xen’s security philosophy.
For optimal security, we recommend the use of physically isolated network ports for guest
traffic. Physical separation provides more robust isolation compared to using a dedicated
VLAN, though the latter can still be a suitable option for balancing simplicity with security
needs.
In practice, a fully dedicated and isolated approach involves connecting a dedicated NIC on
the host to a specific port on your switch. You should then establish a Network on this NIC
without configuring an IP for the Dom0 NIC, ensuring that your Dom0 has no direct access
to the VM network. This setup allows the network to remain completely untrusted. When
creating VMs, this network should be exclusively used.
Furthermore, consider the potential risks such as fake DHCP attacks within this untrusted
network. Adequate planning is necessary to safeguard other guests within the same net-
work.
Lastly, for a more robust defense-in-depth strategy, it is advisable to provide two separate
virtual networks for your VMs: one for management purposes (e.g., SSH or other remote ac-
cess tools) and another for exposing services to the Internet. Ensure that the VM manage-
ment network is distinct from the XCP-ng management network to enhance overall network
security.

Port Locking Port locking is a critical security feature that combats ARP and IP spoofing

network traffic, effectively isolating the unauthorized attempt.

this process, refer to the XCP-ng’s documentation.
Network traffic rules Traffic rules let you filter the traffic of your VMs directly in the virtual
switch of each host (Open vSwitch), before it reaches the physical network or another VM.
They are a good complement to the firewalls of your guests and of your physical network,
especially to isolate VMs sharing the same subnet or VLAN.
Traffic rules are managed by the Xen Orchestra SDN Controller plugin, and applied on each
host by a dedicated XAPI plugin. A rule can be set on a single virtual interface (VIF) or
on a whole network, and works on any network: physical NICs, bonds, VLANs or private
networks. For example, a rule can drop all TCP traffic on port 5060 (SIP) from a given VM
towards another IP address, or restrict a whole network to the few protocols it really needs.

use them, the SDN Controller must run in XAPI plugin mode (useDirectChannel = false
in the Xen Orchestra configuration). The previous implementation, which sent OpenFlow
messages directly to Open vSwitch, only supported VIFs on the management network, with-
out VLANs or bonds: if you still use it, follow the migration guide to move your rules to the
new backend.

3.3 Storage protection
Virtualized data storage, characterized by a dissociation between physical storage devices
and their virtual counterparts, effectively simplifies complexity from the user’s perspective.
This abstraction can be seen when a single physical storage device is represented as mul-
tiple virtual storage devices, or when several physical storage units are consolidated into a
single virtual disk. Despite these benefits, it is crucial to maintain rigorous security proto-
cols to protect virtualized storage.
It is advisable to allocate separate network interfaces for storage, distinct from those used
for management traffic. These dedicated storage NICs should operate on a different IP
subnet than that used for the main administrative interfaces, which can be configured us-
ing tools like Xen Orchestra. Additionally, bonding multiple NICs can enhance resilience.
For environments using IP-based storage solutions, such as NFS or iSCSI, it’s important to
segregate storage traffic from management traffic to prevent any potential crossover in the
XCP-ng control domain.
Furthermore, depending on the type of storage deployed, implementing target and host au-
thentication is recommended to secure data against internal threats. This level of security
is particularly crucial if the storage network could be accessed by less secure parts of the
organization. Unauthorized access could lead to data interception that might compromise
not only the integrity of the virtual environment but also the confidentiality of sensitive in-
formation stored within the VMs. Such preemptive measures help fortify the storage infras-
tructure against potential internal and external threats.
Finally, organizations looking to safeguard their virtualized storage should be mindful that
these storage units might span several physical locations. The security of the storage setup
must take into account all locations where the physical devices are housed to prevent phys-
ical threats or vulnerabilities. For instance, when expanding storage capacity—perhaps by
adding a Storage Repository (SR) in a new location due to space constraints—it is essential
to ensure that this new location is as secure as the original.
3.3.1 General recommendations
Adhering to structured and systematic naming and describing conventions for Storage Repos-
itories (SR) is a vital practice in storage management. Incorporating details such as their
role and physical location into their names ensures clarity and consistency, which is crucial
for efficient management and swift decision-making in environments with complex storage
setups. For example, names like Prod_SSD_Chicago or Test_HDD_NY provide immedi-
ate insights into the SR’s usage (production or test), the type of disks (SSD, NVMe or HDD),
and its physical location. This enhanced clarity supports improved operational efficiency by
streamlining administrative tasks such as allocation, monitoring, and maintenance, allow-
ing administrators to quickly manage resources without delving into deeper configuration
details.
Furthermore, a predictable naming convention facilitates automation and scripting, reduc-
ing manual workloads and minimizing the chances of errors such as misconfigurations or
incorrect data allocations. Consistent naming and detailed descriptions also bolster secu-

3.3.2 Local Storage
Local storage repositories in a virtualized environment refer to storage directly attached to
the physical host that runs the virtual machines. Using local storage can significantly impact
security in various ways.
Firstly, local storage inherently limits data access to the physical server on which it re-
sides, potentially increasing data security by reducing the attack vectors available to ex-
ternal threats compared to network-based storage options. However, this setup can also
concentrate risk; if the physical server is compromised, the data stored locally is at high
risk of being accessed or damaged.
Additionally, local storage does not naturally support advanced features like fast live VM
migration (with memory only), which can complicate disaster recovery and business conti-
nuity plans. Despite these security benefits of limiting data access to a single server, local
storage is susceptible to data loss due to hardware failures, user errors, or malicious at-
tacks. Therefore, it is crucial to implement regular and secure backup procedures that align
with organizational data protection policies.
Backups should be stored securely, ideally offsite or on separate systems, to mitigate the
risks associated with localized data threats. For a detailed discussion on securing back-
ups and adhering to best practices, refer to the backup security section of this document.
This section provides essential guidelines on implementing robust backup strategies that
protect data integrity and availability, even when utilizing local storage solutions.
Local storage redundancy within XCP-ng environments can be achieved through two pri-
mary methods: hardware RAID and software RAID. Both approaches provide crucial re-
dundancy to protect data in the event of disk failure, thereby enhancing the reliability and
availability of your storage infrastructure. Hardware RAID, managed by dedicated RAID con-
trollers, typically offers faster performance and offloads processing from the host machine.
In contrast, software RAID is managed by the host’s operating system, providing a more
cost-effective and flexible solution without the need for specialized hardware. Regardless
of the chosen method, it is vital to regularly monitor the health of the RAID configuration.
This proactive monitoring ensures that any issues, such as a failing disk, are identified and
addressed promptly, allowing for timely replacements and maintenance. Maintaining the
RAID system’s health is crucial for ensuring that it continues to provide the necessary pro-
tection and performance to support your virtualized environment.
3.3.3 Shared Storage: NFS
In managing a file-based NFS storage repository, it is imperative to adopt robust security
practices to safeguard the mounted NFS repository on the XCP-ng control domain. Each
storage repository within this setup is represented as a directory on the remote NFS server,
where each virtual disk is stored as a file: VHD, or QCOW2 for the disks created in this format

To enhance security, strict access controls must be implemented. By configuring NFS export

Additionally, it is crucial to maintain the isolation of the storage interfaces within a dedicated

configured with care.
3.3.4 Shared Storage: SMB
When managing an SMB storage network that serves both XCP-ng pools and potentially
non-XCP-ng systems, it’s advisable to use VLANs to segregate your SMB storage traffic.
This strategy ensures that each segment of your network is isolated, enhancing security
and reducing the risk of unauthorized access.
Furthermore, if your infrastructure includes multiple XCP-ng pools, it is crucial to avoid shar-
ing SMB storage networks among pools that operate under different security levels. For in-
stance, a production server pool, which likely contains sensitive and critical data, should not
share its storage network with a test server pool that might have lower security standards.
To enhance security measures further, we strongly recommend implementing SMB user-
name/password host authentication. This adds an additional layer of security by ensuring
that only authorized hosts can access the SMB storage, thereby protecting your data from
potential unauthorized access or breaches. This practice is essential in maintaining the in-
tegrity and confidentiality of your storage network.
3.3.5 Shared Storage: iSCSI
XCP-ng employs the OpenISCSI software initiator to facilitate connections to iSCSI targets
for handling iSCSI traffic. In environments where iSCSI storage networks are shared among
various XCP-ng pools or alongside non-XCP-ng systems, it’s advisable to employ VLANs to
segregate your iSCSI storage traffic effectively. This separation helps in maintaining orga-
nized and secure network traffic flows. You can isolate further by using a fully dedicated
physical network (cf the Network part of this guide).
When dealing with multiple XCP-ng pools, it is particularly important to consider the trust
levels of each pool before allowing them to share an iSCSI storage network. For instance,
it is not advisable for a pool designated for production servers to share its storage network

Moreover, enhancing the security of your iSCSI setup is crucial. Implementing CHAP (Challenge-

strengthens the security posture. This authentication method ensures that only authorized
devices and users can access the iSCSI network, thereby safeguarding sensitive data from

In addition to these security measures, employing multipath can greatly enhance the re-

3.3.6 Shared Storage: Fibre Channel
We strongly advise the implementation of LUN masking within your SAN to enhance the
security of Fibre Channel traffic. LUN masking is a technique that restricts data access to
specified Host Bus Adapters (HBAs), making a Logical Unit Number (LUN) accessible only
to certain hosts. This method prevents the LUN from being detected during scans by unau-
thorized hosts, thereby securing your storage environment. By precisely configuring which
LUNs are visible to which hosts, LUN masking offers a tailored approach to controlling ac-
cess to the volumes on your SAN based on your specific HBA configuration.
In addition to LUN masking, creating zones within your SAN is recommended to further se-
cure and manage Fibre Channel traffic. SAN zoning allows you to logically group Fibre Chan-
nel devices, separating them based on the storage fabric’s physical layout. This separation
is crucial, especially in isolating storage traffic from servers that may be more exposed to se-
curity risks, such as web servers that handle significant amounts of Internet traffic. Zoning
not only prevents devices outside a zone from detecting those within but also controls traffic
between devices through the Fibre Channel switch, effectively serving as an access control
mechanism within SANs. This method enhances security by isolating the traffic within each
zone, limiting potential damage in the event of a network breach and reducing the overall
attack surface.

4 XCP-ng Protection
Xen operates as a type-1 hypervisor, known for providing a high degree of isolation and pos-
sessing a robust security architecture compared to other hypervisors[5]. However, despite
its advanced security features, Xen is not devoid of vulnerabilities. As hypervisors form the
underlying base for numerous systems, addressing these security concerns is increasingly
vital[15].
This section compiles established best practices for those aiming to achieve a security level
that meets their specific needs. Whether implementing fundamental security measures or
opting for physical isolation for particular workloads, it is crucial for you to assess the risks
involved and determine the most appropriate security approach for your situation.
4.1 XCP-ng deployment
Before actually installing XCP-ng, it’s vital to emphasize the importance of considering se-
curity right from the design phase, even before the actual installation of XCP-ng begins.
Integrating security considerations at the earliest stages of planning and deployment en-
sures that protective measures are ingrained in the infrastructure, rather than being tacked
on as an afterthought. This proactive approach to security is crucial because it helps to iden-
tify and mitigate potential vulnerabilities from the outset, significantly reducing the risk of
future breaches and attacks.
4.1.1 Pre-flight check
Before proceeding with the installation of XCP-ng on your chosen hardware, it’s crucial to
ensure that the foundational elements of your systems are fully prepared and secure. A
critical initial step is to apply all recommended firmware updates, including BIOS/UEFI up-
dates, provided by your hardware vendor. This ensures that your machines are up-to-date
with the latest security patches and performance improvements, minimizing vulnerabilities
that could be exploited during or after the installation of XCP-ng.
In addition to updating your system firmware, establish a clear understanding of how your
hardware vendor communicates about future updates. Knowing the channels through which
security and stability notifications are provided, whether via email alerts, RSS feeds, or ven-
dor portals, is essential for maintaining the ongoing security of your systems. This proactive
approach not only enhances the initial security posture of your deployment but also sets a
protocol for routine maintenance and updates.
CPU microcode deserves a special mention, as many hardware vulnerabilities (such as the
AMD Zen 2 opcode cache issue described in VSA-2026-015) are fixed, or only fully miti-
gated, by a microcode update. XCP-ng ships updated Intel and AMD microcode packages
as a convenience, but updating the firmware of your servers remains the preferred method:
any newer microcode found in the firmware takes precedence over the one provided by XCP-
ng. Some microcode updates also have prerequisites on the firmware side (for instance, re-
cent AMD microcode requires a firmware supporting the "Entrysign" fix), which is one more

Before initiating the installation of XCP-ng, it is essential to consult the official documen-

misconfigurations or unsupported installation practices that could compromise the stability
and security of your deployment. The official documentation provides detailed guidance1 on
the necessary hardware specifications, supported configurations, and best practices for a
successful installation. You can also check your servers, network cards, storage controllers

4.1.2 Installation
Ensuring the integrity of your XCP-ng installation is paramount to safeguard against supply
chain attacks, which can compromise your system before it even runs. To guarantee that
you are installing an official and unaltered version of XCP-ng, begin by downloading the
installation files directly from the official website at https://xcp-ng.org/download/.
Use the most recent installation ISO: refreshed XCP-ng 8.3 LTS images are published regu-
larly (the latest in August 2026) and already contain most of the updates released since 8.3
came out, which reduces the exposure window between installation and the first update.
To verify the authenticity of the downloaded ISO file, check its GPG signature and compare
its SHA256 fingerprint with the one provided next to the download. This step is crucial for
confirming that the file has not been tampered with or altered in any way.
In addition to the integrity check of the ISO, it’s important to note that all packages within
the XCP-ng ecosystem are digitally signed using GPG. This adds an extra layer of security by
ensuring that the packages have not been modified post-signature. During the installation
process, make sure to select the "Verify Installation Source" option. This enables the sys-
tem to check the digital signatures of the installation packages, providing reassurance that
you are using genuine and secure software components. By following these steps, you can
significantly minimize the risk of introducing compromised software into your environment,
maintaining a secure and reliable virtualization platform.
4.2 Updates
Staying up-to-date with the latest security updates is critical for maintaining the integrity
and security of any system, especially as cybersecurity threats rapidly evolve. Security up-
dates for XCP-ng are published as soon as they are ready, alongside regular maintenance
updates. Their pace has increased significantly in 2026, partly due to the rise of AI-assisted
security research: it is now common to see two or three security batches in a single month.
Plan your maintenance windows accordingly, rather than expecting a single monthly up-
date. To ensure the reliability of these updates, each one undergoes thorough testing be-
fore release, guaranteeing that they do not introduce new issues while solving existing ones.
Security updates are never gated behind a paid support contract: every user receives them.

4.3 Pool securisation
In XCP-ng, utilizing a pool allows for the centralization of network and storage configura-
tions, which significantly simplifies operations such as live migration and Rolling Pool Up-
date (RPU). This centralized approach ensures that configuration changes or updates are
uniformly applied across all hosts within the pool, enhancing consistency and reducing op-
erational complexity. However, it is crucial that the exchange of information between hosts
within the pool occurs over a secure and isolated network. As this typically involves the
management network, it is advisable to refer back to the recommendations provided in the
previous Network section to ensure that these communications are adequately protected.
To further enhance security, especially when dealing with sensitive data or critical applica-
tions, additional isolation between groups of VMs—or even individual VMs—can be imple-
mented. This can be achieved by configuring separate pools for different groups of VMs,
each with its own dedicated network and storage. This strategy not only segregates the
data and management traffic of different VM groups but also minimizes the risk of cross-
contamination or unauthorized access, embodying the principle of defense in depth. Such
physical isolation not only reinforces security but also provides robust fault tolerance and
performance benefits, making it an essential component of a comprehensive security strat-
egy in virtualized environments.

certificates (xe pool-certificate-sync) rather than weakening the TLS verification,
and as a general rule, always fully update a host before upgrading it or joining it to a pool.
Finally, XAPI offers its own role-based access control, which can be combined with Active
Directory accounts directly at the pool level. This feature is not used by Xen Orchestra or
XO Lite, and several privilege escalation vulnerabilities were fixed in it in 2026 (VSA-2026-
011). Unless you have a specific need for it, do not join your pools to Active Directory nor
grant XAPI roles to directory accounts: manage your users and their permissions in Xen
Orchestra instead, and keep direct access to XAPI restricted to Xen Orchestra and a few
administrators.
4.4 Control Domain securisation
In every host within a pool, there exists a control domain, which is a specialized and privi-
leged virtual machine (VM) that provides essential low-level services to other VMs. These
services include access to physical devices and running the management tool stack. The
control domain is meticulously designed with only the necessary functionality required to
manage the host effectively.
To ensure the security, integrity, and supportability of the control domain, it is imperative
that no modifications are made unless explicitly recommended. Adding non-recommended
changes can jeopardize the stability and security of the entire system. This also applies to
software and accounts: do not install additional services or create additional local users
in the control domain. In 2026, a series of Linux kernel local privilege escalation vulnera-
bilities ("Copy Fail" and its successors) affected the control domain kernel. In the XCP-ng
threat model, their impact is limited precisely because they require an unprivileged pro-
cess to already run in the control domain: keeping dom0 minimal is what keeps this class
of vulnerabilities out of reach.
Furthermore, we advise setting robust passwords for the control domain and maintaining
strict control over who has access to these passwords. All the hosts of a pool share the
same root password: change it from the pool master with xe user-password-change,
so it is applied to the whole pool. It is equally important to implement a stringent policy for
changing both the pool secret and the root account passwords of control domains whenever
individuals with access leave the organization. This proactive approach helps safeguard the
control domain against unauthorized access and potential security breaches, ensuring that
the foundational management layer of your infrastructure remains secure.

XCP-ng is a robust virtualization platform that utilizes the Xen hypervisor to manage and

which includes various management and operational tools. Due to its elevated privileges

4.4.2 Time synchronization via NTP
We strongly advocate for synchronizing all systems within a geographic region to the same
time source, such as an NTP server. This synchronization is crucial when you are investi-
gating events and changes across different systems. Having a unified time setting across
all systems simplifies the process of searching and correlating events through system logs.
It allows for a more straightforward and effective analysis, as the logs from various sys-
tems will all reflect the same time frame. Accurate time is also a security requirement in
itself: TLS certificates, used between hosts and by Xen Orchestra, are rejected when clocks
disagree, and authentication tokens rely on correct expiration times. NTP servers can be
configured during installation, from xsconsole, or through the XAPI API on recent XCP-ng
8.3 versions.
Additionally, when reviewing log entries, it is important to be cognizant of the time zones.
Ensure you understand whether a log entry records time in local time or in Coordinated
Universal Time (UTC)/Greenwich Mean Time (GMT). This awareness prevents confusion and
ensures accuracy in tracking and interpreting system changes and events, enhancing the
reliability of your log data as a source for troubleshooting and security auditing.
4.4.3 Remote syslog server

ensuring that they remain an unaltered and reliable record of all system activities.
This setup is crucial for effective incident response as it provides a trustworthy source for
forensic analysis, helping to accurately determine the scope, impact, and origin of secu-

administrators are prevented from altering logs to cover up misconfigurations or malicious

4.4.4 Disable HTTP
By default, XCP-ng hosts answer on port 80 (HTTP) in addition to port 443 (HTTPS). Unless
a legacy client really needs it, port 80 should be disabled, so that every connection to the
management API is encrypted. This is a per-host setting, available from the command line
or the API.
To disable port 80 / HTTP on a host, connect to its console and run:
xe host-param-set https-only=true uuid=<uuid of the host>
Please make sure all the clients connecting to the host are doing so using HTTPS already,
and are able to trust the certificates used by the host.
If a client was missed and can’t be fixed immediately, the change can be reverted by running:
xe host-param-set https-only=false uuid=<uuid of the host>
The UUID of the host can be discovered by pressing tab after typing uuid= in the terminal.
Because the command is host-specific, it needs to be run for all hosts in a pool that needs
securing.
4.4.5 Disable SSH
Disabling SSH on XCP-ng and particularly within Dom0 can significantly enhance the secu-

tion, opens a potential gateway for remote attacks if not properly secured. In the context
of Dom0, which is a privileged domain with extensive access to the hardware and the tool-
stack, the risks are amplified.
By disabling SSH, you effectively close off a common attack vector used by cyber attackers
to gain unauthorized access. This action reduces the surface area susceptible to external
threats, particularly from sophisticated network-based attacks aiming to exploit weak or
compromised credentials.
Xen Orchestra (XOA), the orchestration tool for XCP-ng (and part of Vates VMS), does not
rely on SSH for communication with hosts. This means that even with SSH disabled, you

4.5 Physical securisation
Securing the physical access to servers is a critical aspect of comprehensive data center
security, yet it is often overlooked in favor of software-based security measures. Physical
security is essential because it forms the first line of defense against unauthorized access,
theft, or damage to hardware that could potentially lead to data breaches or disruptions in
service.
4.5.1 Supply chain
When considering the physical securitization of servers, supply chain security is a crucial
aspect that demands attention, whether purchasing directly from a provider or acquiring
second-hand refurbished equipment. For direct purchases from a provider, it is impera-
tive to choose reputable and trustworthy suppliers who have stringent security measures in
place throughout their supply chain. This includes ensuring that the hardware components
are sourced from reliable manufacturers and that the servers are assembled, transported,
and delivered securely to prevent tampering or unauthorized access. Verification of the in-
tegrity of the hardware through supply chain audits and compliance with industry standards
is essential to mitigate risks.
On the other hand, acquiring second-hand refurbished servers introduces additional secu-
rity considerations. It is vital to work with certified refurbishers who follow rigorous proce-
dures for data sanitization and hardware refurbishment. These refurbishers should provide
detailed documentation of the server’s history, including previous ownership and mainte-
nance records, to ensure transparency. Physical inspection and testing of the refurbished
servers are necessary to confirm that no malicious hardware modifications have been made.
Furthermore, implementing secure transport and delivery mechanisms for refurbished servers
is crucial to prevent interception or tampering during transit.
4.5.2 Physical access
Best practices in physical server security include housing servers in a locked, access-controlled
environment, using surveillance cameras to monitor these areas, and maintaining strict ac-
cess logs that record every entry and exit. Implementing biometric security measures such
as fingerprint or retinal scans can further enhance security by ensuring that only authorized
personnel can access sensitive hardware.
Additionally, environmental controls should be in place to protect equipment from fire, flood-
ing, and other hazards. While software hardening is crucial for defending against cyber
threats, physical security measures prevent direct interference with the hardware, mak-
ing it an indispensable element of a well-rounded security strategy. By neglecting physical
security, organizations leave a critical vulnerability exposed, potentially undermining even
the most sophisticated software security efforts.

4.5.3 Remote management/IPMI
Remote management systems for servers, such as IPMI (Intelligent Platform Management
Interface), play a crucial role in managing and monitoring server hardware environments re-
motely. However, securing these systems is paramount because, while they offer significant
operational efficiencies, they can also pose serious security risks if not properly protected.
IPMI allows administrators to manage system health, monitor system logs, and even re-
cover or reboot servers remotely, which could be catastrophic if accessed by unauthorized
users.
A BMC (IPMI, iDRAC, iLO. . . ) must never be reachable from the Internet: these interfaces
have a long history of critical, pre-authentication vulnerabilities, and are actively scanned
for. Reach them through the same VPN or Zero Trust access as the rest of your manage-
ment plane (see the section "Never expose Xen Orchestra to the Internet"). Best practices
for securing IPMI also include using dedicated network interfaces that are isolated from the
main production network to reduce exposure to external threats. Additionally, strong pass-
word policies and regular updates are essential to guard against brute-force attacks and
vulnerabilities. Implementing IP and MAC address filtering can further restrict access to
the management interface to only known and authorized systems. Enabling encryption for
IPMI sessions prevents eavesdropping and ensures that sensitive data transmitted during
remote management sessions is protected.
Neglecting the security of IPMI and similar management interfaces can lead to significant
security breaches, allowing attackers deep control over physical hardware without needing
physical access. Therefore, it is just as critical to secure these remote management tools
with robust practices as it is to secure the servers they manage. This dual focus ensures
that both the physical and remote management layers contribute to a secure and resilient
infrastructure.
4.5.4 Decommissioning servers
Decommissioning XCP-ng hosts involves several critical security measures to ensure that
sensitive data is comprehensively erased and the system is safely removed from your in-
frastructure. Start by thoroughly sanitizing all storage devices associated with the host,
including virtual machine disks and any connected backup media. This data sanitization
should adhere to industry standards such as those outlined by NIST to ensure data cannot
be recovered or reconstructed.
Additionally, all configuration and log files on the host that may contain sensitive operational
data should be securely deleted to prevent potential exploitation.
Once the host is cleared of sensitive data and services, update all relevant operational doc-
umentation and inventory systems to reflect the removal of the decommissioned host. This
step is crucial to maintain accurate records and prevent future security gaps.
Finally, maintain a comprehensive audit trail of the decommissioning process. Documenting
steps like data sanitization, hardware disposal, and software deactivation provides a valu-
able record that supports compliance audits and security assessments, ensuring that the

5 Xen Orchestra Protection
This section is dedicated to outlining the best practices and key recommendations for se-
curing your Xen Orchestra Virtual Appliance (XOA). As the central management platform for
your virtual environments, ensuring the security and integrity of XOA is paramount. Here,
we will explore a variety of strategies and measures designed to safeguard your Xen Or-
chestra installation from potential threats and vulnerabilities, ensuring it operates safely
and efficiently within your IT infrastructure.
5.1 Never expose Xen Orchestra to the Internet
Important
Xen Orchestra, the XCP-ng management interfaces (XAPI and XO Lite), XO proxies,
server BMCs and backup repositories must never be reachable from the Internet. Re-
mote administration must go through an authenticated access layer that is separate
from Xen Orchestra: a VPN or a Zero Trust access gateway, landing in a dedicated ad-
ministration network.
Xen Orchestra holds the keys to your whole infrastructure: the credentials of every pool
and backup repository, the VM consoles, and the ability to delete VMs together with their
backups. An attacker who takes control of it controls your production and, in the same move,
your ability to recover. This is true of the whole management plane. Its web interfaces, its
APIs (REST, JSON-RPC and console WebSockets) and the management interfaces of your
hosts must not be reachable from the Internet in any form.
5.1.1 Why the bar has been raised
Previous versions of this guide accepted an Internet-facing Xen Orchestra, provided it was
protected with strong authentication. We no longer do, because AI-assisted offensive op-
erations have changed what an exposed interface means:
• The time between the publication of a fix and a working exploit has collapsed. AI tools
can analyze a patch, find the vulnerability it fixes and produce an exploit in hours. That
is shorter than any realistic maintenance window, including yours.
• Vulnerabilities are being found faster, by defenders and attackers alike. The increased
pace of security updates described in the Updates section is one consequence of this.
Some flaws will be found and exploited before any fix exists.
• Reconnaissance and exploitation are automated at scale. AI-orchestrated intrusion
campaigns that carry out most of their steps autonomously have been documented
publicly [3], and national agencies expect AI to keep reducing the time needed to ex-
ploit vulnerabilities [12]. An exposed management interface is found, identified and
attacked within hours of going online, not weeks.
• Authentication is not a sufficient barrier. VSA-2026-044 allowed any authenticated
Xen Orchestra user to become an administrator, and pre-authentication vulnerabili-

ties in Internet-facing management interfaces are routinely exploited across the whole
industry. Multi-factor authentication protects the login form, not the code that runs be-
fore it.
On a private network, the same vulnerability first requires the attacker to get inside. That
buys you the time to apply the fix. On the Internet, there is no such margin.
This position matches the CISA Binding Operational Directive 23-02, which requires man-
agement interfaces to be removed from the Internet or placed behind a Zero Trust policy
enforcement point separate from the interface itself [9]. It also matches the ANSSI recom-
mendations on secure administration, built around a dedicated administration network and
dedicated administration workstations [2].
5.1.2 Recommended access methods
The following methods can be combined. The first one is the foundation, and the others are
ways to reach it remotely.
A dedicated administration network Place Xen Orchestra, the management interfaces of
your hosts, XO proxies and BMCs in a dedicated administration network: a separate VLAN
or, better, a separate physical network. Filter it so that only administration workstations
can reach it, and only on the ports they need (HTTPS to Xen Orchestra, for example). Ide-
ally, these workstations are dedicated to administration, with no e-mail and no general web
browsing, so that a phishing attack on an administrator does not land directly on your man-
agement plane.
A VPN with strong authentication For remote administrators, a VPN into the administra-
tion network is the most common and proven method. Follow these practices:
• Prefer modern protocols with a small attack surface, such as WireGuard or IPsec (IKEv2),
over legacy SSL VPN products. WireGuard does not answer unauthenticated packets,
so it is invisible to scanners.
• Require multi-factor authentication, ideally phishing-resistant: FIDO2 security keys,
or certificates stored on managed devices.
• Give each administrator their own credentials, never a shared account or a shared key,
and revoke them as part of the departure process.
• Use a dedicated VPN profile for administrators. It should only route to the adminis-
tration network, with firewall rules limited to the services they need, rather than give
access to the whole internal network.
• Send connection logs to your central logging system and alert on unusual connections
(new device, unusual hours or source country).
Zero Trust Network Access A Zero Trust Network Access (ZTNA) solution, also called
an identity-aware proxy, authenticates the user and checks the device (managed device,
up-to-date system, MFA) before any packet reaches Xen Orchestra [13]. Its connectors
usually open outbound connections only, so no inbound port has to be opened on your site.
Both open-source and commercial solutions exist, including mesh VPNs with an identity
provider and access policies. CISA considers this the preferred option. If the broker is a

cloud service, keep in mind that it may terminate TLS and see the administration traffic:
check that this is compatible with your policies.
A bastion for privileged sessions When administrators need SSH access to the hosts or
to the appliance, or when regulations require session recording, use a bastion (jump host),
possibly part of a Privileged Access Management (PAM) solution. The bastion is itself only
reachable through the VPN or the ZTNA, never directly from the Internet.
Site-to-site tunnels between sites For multi-site infrastructures, connect Xen Orchestra

links, not through public endpoints (see the Proxies section).
5.1.3 What is not a substitute
The following measures are sometimes presented as a way to keep Xen Orchestra on the
Internet. None of them changes the fact that Xen Orchestra’s code is reachable by anyone:
• Port forwarding (NAT) to Xen Orchestra, even on a non-standard port. Internet-wide
scanners cover every port.
• A reverse proxy or a web application firewall that just forwards the traffic. A reverse
proxy only helps if it authenticates the user itself, before forwarding anything, which
makes it a ZTNA gateway.
• An allowlist of public source IP addresses. It is acceptable as an additional layer or as
a temporary measure. But it does not identify a person, and addresses can be shared
(carrier-grade NAT, cloud providers) or change.
• Geographic blocking, port knocking or a hard-to-guess host name. These are security
through obscurity (see the "Security through transparency" section). Note that pub-
lic certificates, such as the ones issued by Let’s Encrypt, are published in Certificate
Transparency logs, so host names are not secret anyway.
• The multi-factor authentication and login rate limiting of Xen Orchestra. They are nec-
essary, but they do not protect against a vulnerability that can be exploited before or
around authentication.
5.1.4 Securing the access gateway
The VPN or ZTNA gateway becomes the only exposed entry point of your management
plane. It will be targeted accordingly: VPN appliances and other edge devices are among
the most exploited products of recent years. Treat it as your most exposed asset:
• Apply its security updates within days, and subscribe to its vendor’s security advi-
sories.
• Never expose the management interface of the gateway itself on the Internet.
• Place the gateway in a DMZ, not in the administration network, so that its compromise
does not give direct access to Xen Orchestra and the hosts.
• Keep its configuration minimal, and monitor its logs.

5.1.5 Specific cases
• Customers, self-service users and MSPs: users of the self-service portal, customers
and external providers must go through the same VPN or ZTNA, with their own ac-
counts. If you need a public portal for your customers, build a separate application on
top of the REST API. It should expose only the actions your customers need, with a
dedicated token and minimal RBAC roles, instead of publishing Xen Orchestra.
• Certificates: you do not need to expose Xen Orchestra to obtain a trusted certificate.
Use the DNS-01 challenge of Let’s Encrypt, or a certificate issued by your internal cer-
tificate authority (see Certificates Integrity).
• Updates and support: XOA only needs outbound access to receive its updates (see
Deployment recommendations). No inbound port is needed.
• AI assistants: run the MCP server inside your administration network. Never open Xen
Orchestra to the Internet so that a cloud-hosted agent can reach it (see AI assistants
and MCP).
• Emergency access: plan a break-glass access in advance, such as a secondary VPN on
an out-of-band link, or a cellular modem on the administration network. A port opened
"temporarily" during an incident tends to stay open.
5.1.6 Verify from the outside
Check regularly what is really exposed. Scan your public IP ranges from an external net-
work, and use attack surface monitoring tools or Internet-wide scanning services (such as
Shodan or Censys) to see what they index. No Xen Orchestra, XO proxy, XCP-ng host, XO
Lite or BMC should ever answer. Repeat the check after every network change.
If you find that Xen Orchestra or a host has been exposed, remove the exposure first, then
treat the system as potentially compromised. Review the Xen Orchestra audit log for un-
known users, tokens and actions, apply all pending security updates, and rotate the cre-
dentials it holds: Xen Orchestra users and tokens, pool credentials, backup repository cre-
dentials and plugin secrets.

5.4 Xen Orchestra Update Cycle

5.5 XOA Operating System Update Cycle
Xen Orchestra (XOA) is built on Debian. By default, XOA utilizes unattended-updates, a
feature in Debian that automatically installs security updates without user intervention. This
ensures that the system remains secure by promptly applying critical patches that address
vulnerabilities as soon as they are available.
Note that a kernel update only becomes effective after the appliance is restarted. When
a kernel vulnerability affects XOA (as was the case with the "Copy Fail" vulnerability, VSA-

to reboot the XOA VM to run it. Schedule regular reboots of the appliance, for instance right

With each new major release of Debian, we provide a corresponding new version of XOA that

because direct distribution upgrades (using commands like dist-upgrade) between major

advisable to redeploy XOA with the new base version.

downtime and making it easy to maintain a current, secure, and stable XOA environment.

5.6 Credential database encryption
Xen Orchestra stores its records in a local Redis database: connections to your pools (in-
cluding their credentials), backup jobs and repositories, users, groups, authentication to-
kens, ACLs, plugin configurations, and so on. This makes it a valuable target for an attacker
who gains access to the appliance’s disk or to a copy of it.
Since Xen Orchestra 6.5, this database can be encrypted at rest (AES-256-GCM), by set-
ting encryptCredentialDatabase = true in the [redis] section of the configuration
file. The encryption key is derived from two halves stored in different places: one in Xen-
Store, provided by the host running the XOA VM, and one in a local file readable only by
root. Neither the appliance’s disk alone, nor its XenStore data alone, is enough to decrypt
the database. We recommend enabling it, while keeping the following points in mind:
• Xen Orchestra must run as a VM on an XCP-ng host, with the guest tools available, as
one half of the key lives in XenStore.
• Once encryption is enabled, exporting the Xen Orchestra configuration requires a passphrase,
and the export is encrypted with it. Export a fresh configuration backup right after en-
abling encryption, and store it securely: it contains your credentials, in a form protected
only by this passphrase.
• Never back up the key halves in plaintext, and never restart Xen Orchestra if one of
them has been lost: restore it first, or restore a configuration backup.
Whether encryption is enabled or not, Redis must only listen on the local interface, as it
does by default. Changing this setting would expose the whole Xen Orchestra state to the
network.

The importance of using strong passwords in Xen Orchestra cannot be overstated, even
though the platform incorporates a "cool off" feature that limits the number of login at-
tempts per minute. This security measure helps mitigate brute-force attacks but should not
be solely relied upon as the primary line of defense. Strong passwords are a fundamental
security practice that significantly enhances the protection of user accounts and sensitive

In addition to robust password policies, Xen Orchestra supports enhanced security through
Two-Factor Authentication (2FA), which adds an extra layer of security beyond just the pass-
word. This can be implemented directly within Xen Orchestra, with a time-based one-time
password (TOTP) configured from each user’s profile, or through external authentication
portals using SAML (Security Assertion Markup Language) or OIDC (OpenID Connect). Pop-

Using hardware tokens for 2FA via OIDC offers a higher level of security compared to tra-

each login attempt, with a secret that never leaves the device and is virtually impossible
to replicate or steal, unlike passwords that can be exposed to theft or loss. This method
significantly reduces the risk of unauthorized access, ensuring that only the holder of the
physical token can access the account. It provides a secure, user-friendly, and reliable way
of protecting access to Xen Orchestra, safeguarding it against potential security breaches
and making it considerably more secure than relying on passwords alone.

5.8 ACLs and RBAC
Xen Orchestra employs a comprehensive ACL (Access Control List) system that allows fine-
tuned control over user permissions and access to various functions within the platform.
At the top of the hierarchy is the admin or "superadmin" user, who possesses full control
over all aspects of Xen Orchestra, including configuration settings, management of backups,
and other critical administrative tasks. This level of access ensures that superadmins can
oversee and manage the entire infrastructure effectively.
For other users, Xen Orchestra adopts a more restrictive approach by default, where new
users are created with no permissions initially. This ensures that access must be intention-
ally granted, rather than automatically assumed, enhancing security. Permissions can be
assigned to these users either directly or through groups, which allows for scalable man-

--- PAGE 44 ---

agement as more users are added. For organizations utilizing Active Directory (AD) or LDAP
directories, Xen Orchestra can integrate with these systems to map groups and permissions
automatically. This integration facilitates streamlined user management and ensures that
permissions are consistently applied according to organizational policies, granting users
access only to the resources they require.
Xen Orchestra 6 introduces a new access control system, RBAC (Role-Based Access Con-
trol, formerly called "ACL v2"), which applies to the REST API and the XO 6 interface, while
the XO 5 interface keeps the original ACLs. RBAC follows a deny-by-default model: non-
admin users start with no access at all, and a deny privilege always takes precedence over
an allow privilege. Permissions are grouped in roles, assigned to users or groups, and each
privilege targets an action (read, start, shutdown, delete. . . ) on a type of resource, optionally
restricted to a subset of objects with a selector (for example, only the VMs tagged qa). Un-
like the original ACLs, RBAC also covers Xen Orchestra objects such as backup jobs, backup
repositories, users and groups.
Built-in role templates cover the most common needs (read only, VM power management,
VM creation, VM administration, network administration, storage administration). The Stor-
age administrator and Network administrator roles, introduced in Xen Orchestra 6.6, are
especially useful to delegate a whole functional area to a team without handing out global
administrator rights. Users and roles can be managed from the Administration tab of XO 6,
or through the REST API.
Keep in mind that every account is part of your attack surface, even one with no permission
at all: VSA-2026-044 described a vulnerability, fixed in Xen Orchestra 6.9, through which
any authenticated user could gain administrator privileges. Only create accounts for peo-
ple who need them, remove them as soon as they no longer do, and apply Xen Orchestra
security updates promptly.
In addition to these user management capabilities, Xen Orchestra offers a self-service fea-
ture designed to abstract the underlying infrastructure from the end-user. This feature al-
lows users to create VMs through a portal that limits visibility to only those resources allo-
cated to them. The self-service portal simplifies the user experience and enforces resource
boundaries, preventing users from accessing or modifying settings outside their purview.
In conclusion, while the superadmin role is powerful, it is advisable to limit this level of ac-
cess to a small number of trusted individuals to minimize potential security risks. Addition-
ally, although Xen Orchestra is designed to manage your entire infrastructure through a sin-
gle interface, for organizations with particularly diverse or geographically dispersed infras-
tructure, it may be beneficial to deploy multiple instances of Xen Orchestra. Each instance
can then manage a specific subset of the infrastructure, allowing for tailored administration
that aligns with local requirements and enhances overall security and performance.

5.11 REST API
The XO REST API provides an efficient way to automate and programmatically control all
authorized actions within the Vates VMS environment. It is now the main Xen Orchestra
API: the XO 6 interface is built on it, and nearly all Xen Orchestra capabilities are exposed
through it. Admin users have access to all endpoints, while non-admin users can use it
within the limits of their RBAC roles. To maintain robust security, it is essential to adhere to
best practices for REST API security.
The REST API accepts two forms of authentication: an authentication token, or a username
and password sent in a Basic Authorization header with every request. For scripts and in-
tegrations, always prefer a token: it does not expose a password, it can be scoped to a
dedicated user with limited roles, and it can be revoked at any time. Reserve Basic authen-
tication for interactive use, and never store passwords in scripts.

Always use HTTPS to ensure that all communications with the API are encrypted, protecting
data in transit from eavesdropping and man-in-the-middle attacks. This is mandatory with
Basic authentication, which sends the credentials with each request. Monitoring and log-
ging API activity are essential practices for detecting and responding to suspicious activities
in real-time. Regular security audits and vulnerability assessments should be conducted to
identify and mitigate potential security issues.
5.11.1 Token Management
Proper token management is critical to securing the XO REST API. Tokens should be de-
signed with a limited lifespan to minimize risk in case of compromise, with expiration times
appropriate to their use case. Each token carries an expiration date, and the maxTokenValidity
setting, in the [authentication] section of the Xen Orchestra configuration file, caps the
validity of every token: lower it to match your security policy. A token can be renewed before
it expires by requesting a new one with the existing token.
Rather than using an administrator token for automation, create a dedicated user for each
integration, give it only the RBAC roles it needs, and create its token with that user: any
user can create its own tokens, and these tokens can only do what the user is allowed to do.
The built-in Swagger UI (/rest/v0/swagger) documents the privileges required by each
endpoint; endpoints without declared privileges are reserved to administrators.
It is vital to implement a workflow for revoking tokens when they are no longer needed
or if they are suspected to be compromised. Also, tokens should have minimal scope and
permissions necessary for their intended use, avoiding excessive permissions that could be
exploited if compromised. Each session should generate unique tokens to enhance security
and avoid reuse.
Regular audits of active tokens should be conducted to ensure there are no tokens with ex-
cessive permissions or that are no longer in use. Tokens must be stored securely, preferably
in encrypted storage mechanisms, and never in local storage or other client-side storage
that can be easily accessed by malicious actors. Ensuring tokens are transmitted securely
via HTTPS and avoiding their inclusion in URLs can also help protect them from being logged
or intercepted.

6 Virtual Machines Protection
Securing virtual machines should follow the same rigorous standards applied to physical
servers, as their security requirements are largely similar. It’s essential to treat the security
of a guest operating system on a virtual machine with the same level of seriousness as you
would a physical machine’s operating system. However, there’s also some extra consider-
ation to keep in mind when operating VMs in your Vates VMS environment.
6.1 Best Practices
Minimizing the attack surface on virtual machines is crucial. Disable any non-essential sys-
tem components at the guest level, adhering to the security guidelines provided by the op-
erating system vendor.
For robust protection, it’s advisable to activate standard security measures on your virtual
machines. This includes installing and maintaining (if applicable) up-to-date antivirus, anti-
spyware, and malware protection. Operating system-level firewalls should also be enabled
if deemed necessary.
To streamline the deployment of secure virtual environments, consider utilizing pre-configured
templates. These templates should have the guest operating system already installed with
optimal security settings. Creating a variety of these templates can further enhance secu-
rity, particularly if they include commonly used applications which are also pre-configured
with appropriate security measures. This approach not only saves time during VM deploy-
ment but also ensures that security configurations are consistently applied, reducing the
likelihood of accidental security oversights.
To further reduce the risks associated with manual configurations and potential human er-
ror, incorporating infrastructure as code (IaC) into your security strategy can be highly effec-
tive. By automating the deployment and management of virtual machines, tools like Ansible,
Packer 5, and Terraform enable consistent and repeatable configurations. This automation
not only streamlines the setup and maintenance processes but also ensures that security
configurations are uniformly applied across all instances. Leveraging these tools helps in
minimizing deviations that can lead to security vulnerabilities, providing a more reliable and
secure infrastructure management approach. By defining your infrastructure and security
settings through code, you can enhance the overall security posture and operational effi-
ciency of your environment.
Finally, after reactivating virtual machines that have been inactive for an extended period,
ensure they are immediately updated with the latest antivirus definitions and any critical
security patches. This step is vital to protect against new vulnerabilities that may have been
identified during the period of dormancy.

6.2 UEFI, Secure Boot and vTPM
Whenever the guest operating system supports it, create your VMs in UEFI mode with Se-
cure Boot enabled. Secure Boot ensures that only signed boot loaders and kernels can start,
which protects the guest against boot-level malware (bootkits). Since the December 2025
updates, XCP-ng 8.3 ships the required certificates by default, including Microsoft’s 2023
certificates, so Guest Secure Boot works out of the box, without having to install certifi-
cates on each pool. The revocation database (dbx) is also updated through regular XCP-ng
updates.
Pools that were set up for Secure Boot with the previous, manual process should switch
to the certificates managed by XCP-ng, and existing VMs need to be updated to receive the
latest certificates, as described in the official documentation6. This matters, because future
Windows installation media will no longer rely on the expiring 2011 certificates.
A virtual TPM (vTPM) can also be attached to UEFI VMs, which is required by Windows 11,
and enables features such as BitLocker disk encryption or measured boot in the guest. Both
Secure Boot and vTPM can be selected when creating a VM from Xen Orchestra 6 or XO Lite.
6.3 Guest tools and templates
Guest tools are drivers and agents running inside your VMs, so they are part of your security
perimeter too. For Windows VMs, use the XCP-ng Windows guest tools: they are signed
by Microsoft since October 2025, compatible with Secure Boot, maintained by Vates (who
also maintain the upstream Xen Windows PV drivers), and available from the guest tools
ISO shipped with XCP-ng. Keep them up to date: version 9.2.385, for example, fixed a
data corruption issue during in-memory snapshots and backups. The legacy XCP-ng 8.2
drivers must no longer be used, as they are affected by unfixed vulnerabilities (XSA-468).
When replacing older drivers, use the XenClean utility from the latest guest tools release,
as earlier versions of XenClean were affected by a DLL sideloading vulnerability (VSA-2026-
012, VSA-2026-040).
Always create VMs from the template matching their operating system. Templates set im-
portant platform options: for instance, Windows templates enable the Viridian (Hyper-V
compatible) enlightenments Windows needs, while the "Other install media" template no
longer enables them by default since October 2026. Conversely, do not enable features a
VM does not need, as each of them adds attack surface: several Xen vulnerabilities in 2025
and 2026 were only reachable through Viridian features, or only with PCI passthrough.

6.7 VM Sizing
Effective resource management and strategic planning are essential for protecting your VMs
from potential disruptions, particularly those caused by Denial-of-service (DoS) attacks.
These attacks aim to overload systems with excessive external communications, thereby
draining the resources necessary for regular operations. Given that VMs often host services
accessible to the outside world, safeguarding them requires a comprehensive approach.
When configuring your VMs, it’s crucial to allocate adequate CPU and memory resources
that align with their operational roles, ensuring they can handle expected workloads ef-
ficiently. This involves not only equipping each VM with the necessary resources to per-
form under normal conditions but also ensuring that they are not overprovisioned to a point
where they could consume an excessive share of host resources during peak times. Such
overprovisioning could lead to resource scarcity for other critical VMs, potentially triggering
a cascade of failures across the system.
To avoid such scenarios, resources should be distributed thoughtfully among VMs to main-
tain balance and prevent any single VM from overwhelming the system. Adjustments to
resource allocation can typically be made during the initial VM setup via configuration tools
like Xen Orchestra, or adjusted post-setup in the VM’s settings, particularly under the Mem-

6.8 Migration and load balancing
Utilizing live migration features like XO Load Balancer plugin and High Availability in virtual
environments necessitates careful consideration of the data sensitivity hosted on the vir-
tual machines. Live migration facilitates the relocation of VMs between hosts, potentially
altering their physical and logical environment. For instance, enabling automatic optimiza-
tions in XO Load Balancer may result in a VM being transferred to a new host without direct
oversight:a typical and intended functionality of this feature.
However, this automation poses risks if VMs with sensitive data are inadvertently moved to
less secure physical locations. For example, a VM could be relocated to a server in a location
that lacks adequate security measures, thus compromising the data’s integrity.
To mitigate these risks, it’s crucial to ensure that every host within the pool adheres to strin-
gent security standards that account for both logical and physical security concerns. This
includes verifying that the physical security of the locations where the hosts are situated is
sufficient to safeguard sensitive data.
Recommendations for managing live migration include:
• Designing pools so that all hosts conform to uniform security criteria.
• Opting out of automatic features in XO Load Balancer to maintain control over where
VMs reside.
• If using automated XO Load Balancing, configure settings to exclude certain hosts from
participating in live migrations to prevent sensitive data from being moved to less se-
cure environments.
Additionally, for scenarios where specific workloads require isolated handling, consider
segregating these workloads to separate pools or specifically excluding certain hosts from
XO Load Balancing algorithms to ensure that these workloads do not migrate to undesired
systems.
The load balancer also supports affinity rules (to keep VMs together) and anti-affinity rules
(to keep the members of a replicated application on separate hosts, so the failure of a single
host never takes down every copy), as well as a cooldown period preventing a VM from
being moved back and forth. Since Xen Orchestra 6.6, every migration decided by the load
balancer appears as a Xen Orchestra task, with the reason of the migration: review them
regularly, so that automatic placement decisions remain traceable.

7 Management and Orchestration
This section offers general guidance on managing Vates VMS, supplementing the specific
advice provided in earlier sections. These additional tips are intended to enhance your over-
all approach to system management within the Vates VMS environment.
7.1 General recommendations
Enforce HTTPS for Secure Communications Always use HTTPS instead of HTTP for ac-
cessing the Xen Orchestra interface. HTTPS encrypts the data transmitted between the
user’s browser and Xen Orchestra, securing it from interception or tampering during trans-
mission. This is crucial for preventing man-in-the-middle attacks and ensuring that sensi-
tive information such as passwords and management commands cannot be easily accessed
by malicious actors.
Restrict Direct Access to XCP-ng APIs Do not expose XCP-ng management APIs directly
to untrusted networks. Instead, ensure that API access is gated through secure, authen-
ticated channels, such as through Xen Orchestra or via secure VPNs. Xen Orchestra itself
must never be exposed to the Internet either: remote administrators reach it through a
VPN or a Zero Trust access gateway (see the section "Never expose Xen Orchestra to the
Internet"). This prevents external parties from attempting to directly interact with the APIs,
mitigating the risk of attacks targeted at the hypervisor management layer. The same ap-
plies to XO Lite, which is served by every host on its management interface: it is meant to
be reached from your administration network, never from the Internet.
Third-party tools connecting directly to XAPI deserve the same scrutiny. For example, the
C# and PowerShell XAPI SDKs did not verify TLS certificates on some of their connections
(XSA-498, VSA-2026-023), exposing session tokens to man-in-the-middle attacks: desk-
top clients and scripts built on these SDKs must be updated or rebuilt against a fixed version.
Xen Orchestra does not use these SDKs and is not affected.
Regularly Update and Patch Maintain a regimen of regular updates and patches for both
Xen Orchestra and XCP-ng. Updates often include critical security patches that protect
against vulnerabilities. Ensure that updates are applied promptly to avoid exposing your
infrastructure to exploits that target known vulnerabilities. Subscribe to the Vates Security
Advisories RSS feed, so that the people in charge of your infrastructure are notified as soon
as an advisory is published, and use the Vates severity rating of each advisory to prioritize
your actions.
Use Network Segmentation and Firewalls Employ network segmentation to isolate man-
agement traffic from general network traffic. Using VLANs or other network partitioning
methods can effectively segregate traffic, reducing the possibility of unauthorized access
to management interfaces. Additionally, configure firewalls to strictly control access to the
management network, allowing only authorized devices and users.

8 Securing your Backups
In Xen Orchestra, implementing robust data protection strategies is fundamental. This en-
compasses the use of backup, replication, and advanced techniques like snapshotting to
ensure that data can be restored in any eventuality. It’s vital to maintain backups in secure,
isolated networks away from the primary data operations, significantly reducing the risk of
data compromise during a breach. This setup also helps in maintaining data integrity by
preventing unauthorized access during the backup process.
We also recommend the NIST Special Publication 800-209 Security Guidelines for Storage
Infrastructure providing relevant guidelines in their dedicated Backup section[8].
8.1 Key Backup Concepts
Full Backup: Captures all data in the system at a specific point in time. While storage-
intensive, it facilitates the most straightforward restoration process.
Incremental Backup: Only backs up changes made since the last backup, whether that
last backup was a full one or an incremental one. This method is less storage-intensive
but requires a more complex restoration process.
Replication: whether full or incremental (formerly called "Disaster Recovery" and "Con-
tinuous Replication"), involves duplicating VM data to an alternative XCP-ng Storage
Repository (SR), usually in another pool. This process is designed to facilitate an im-
mediate startup of the VM from the replicated data in the event that the original stor-
age becomes unavailable. Utilized effectively, replication serves as a robust strategy
for disaster recovery by significantly reducing the Recovery Time Objective (RTO). In-
cremental replication can also run in the reverse direction after a failover, transferring
only the changes made in the meantime.
Backup Repository (BR): A dedicated storage location where backup data is securely stored.
This might be referred to as a "remote" in some contexts.
Mirror Backup: Involves copying a backup repository to another location, providing an ad-
ditional layer of data protection, with a dedicated schedule and retention (eg for archiv-
ing purpose). It exists in full and incremental flavors.
Metadata Backup: Specifically for XCP-ng and Xen Orchestra, this includes backing up the
pool metadata and the Xen Orchestra configuration, which helps restore your infras-
tructure to its correct functional state beyond just the VM data.
Smart Backup: An intelligent backup process that decides the type of backup needed based
on various criteria to optimize the backup process (VM tag, placement. . . )
Immutable Backup: A backup that cannot be modified nor deleted, by anyone, until its re-
tention period has expired. This is the main protection against ransomware and against
an attacker who has compromised the backup infrastructure itself.
The 3-2-1 rule is a good baseline for any backup strategy: keep three copies of your data,
on two different types of media or locations, with one copy off-site. Its more recent variant,
3-2-1-1-0, adds one immutable or air-gapped copy and zero errors, verified by systematic
restoration tests. Xen Orchestra’s features map naturally to both: a single backup job can

8.2 Design Choices
When planning your backup environment within Xen Orchestra, several critical factors must
be considered to ensure the security and reliability of backup operations. Each of these
elements plays a vital role in protecting your data from potential threats and ensuring that
backups can be effectively used for recovery when needed.
Isolating your backup environment is crucial to safeguarding your data. This involves creat-
ing a distinct and secure network segment specifically for backup activities, separate from
the production network. Such isolation helps minimize the risk of cross-contamination from
potential network threats and ensures that backup operations do not interfere with the day-
to-day activities of your primary environment.
Also, strong access control and authentication measures are essential to manage and pro-
tect backups. It’s important to define and enforce who has access to the backup environ-
ment and what level of access they possess. Implementing robust authentication mecha-
nisms, such as multi-factor authentication (MFA), ensures that only authorized personnel
can interact with backup systems and handle sensitive data. This reduces the risk of unau-
thorized access and potential data breaches.
Then, encrypting backup data is an important security measure. Encryption protects data at
rest and in transit, making it unreadable to unauthorized users. Xen Orchestra can encrypt
the content of a backup repository with a key you provide when configuring it: the authen-
ticated encryption algorithm used also guarantees that, at restore time, the data is exactly
what was backed up, or raises an error. Equally important is the secure management of en-
cryption keys. Backup keys should be stored in a secure location, separate from the data
they encrypt, to prevent potential attackers from gaining access to both the keys and the
data simultaneously, and in a place that remains reachable if Xen Orchestra itself is lost.
Some features help with scale or consistency, but are not a protection on their own. Dis-
tributed backups, which spread backup chains over several repositories grouped in a pool,
are a capacity management feature: a distributed job still counts as a single copy in your
strategy. Synchronized snapshots, which take the snapshots of all the VMs of a job (or of a
tag) before transferring any data, give consistent restore points across the VMs of the same
application, such as a database and the services depending on it.
Xen Orchestra configuration backups deserve a special mention, as they contain the cre-
dentials of your pools and backup repositories. Protect them with a passphrase (manda-
tory when credential database encryption is enabled), and store them as carefully as the
backups themselves. Before enabling the XO Config Cloud Backup option, make sure that
sending your configuration to the Vates cloud complies with your policies.
Finally, regular maintenance and updates of all components in the backup chain are vital
to safeguarding your backup environment. This includes updating the Xen Orchestra plat-

kept up-to-date with the latest security patches and firmware updates. Regular updates

8.3 Backup restoration assurance
Backup restoration assurance is a critical component of a robust data protection strategy,
ensuring that backups are not just created but are also viable for recovery. To streamline
and automate the verification of backup integrity and functionality, Vates has implemented
the backup health check feature within Xen Orchestra, also known as Auto Restore Check
(ARC). You can read the official documentation for more details 7.
The ARC process is straightforward and user-friendly. Administrators can simply enable
the "Health check" option within their backup job settings. Once activated, Xen Orchestra
will automatically initiate a restoration test of the backed-up VM. The method of restoration
varies depending on the type of backup: for delta or full backups, Xen Orchestra downloads
and restores the VM; for disaster recovery or continuous replication scenarios, it clones the
VM directly.
Once restored, the system allows a window of up to 10 minutes for the VM to boot up and for
the guest tools to load. The presence of guest tools is crucial as they provide necessary inte-
gration services between the guest operating system and the host, enhancing performance
and management capabilities. If the guest tools do not load within the designated time-
frame, the health check is considered failed, indicating potential issues with the backup’s
integrity or the restore process.
After the check is completed, whether successful or not, the restored VM is automatically
deleted to prevent clutter and conserve resources. This automated health check process
provides a reliable, hands-off method for ensuring that backups are not only present but are
fully operational, capable of restoring the VM to its proper state when needed. This feature
significantly enhances the reliability of the backup system, giving administrators confidence
in their disaster recovery capabilities and the overall resilience of their infrastructure.
A successful backup job is not a proof that the backup can be restored. Automatic health
checks require the guest tools to be installed in the VM; complement them with regular
manual restoration tests of your most critical VMs, ideally including the application layer.
When a VM must be brought back quickly, Xen Orchestra 6.9 can also start a VM directly
from its backup ("live mount"), without copying its disks back first. The live-mounted disks
are read-only and the feature is reserved to administrators: it is a recovery tool, not a re-
placement for a full restore.

8.4 Immutable backups
Ransomware operators know that backups are what stands between them and a ransom:
they routinely try to delete or encrypt them before encrypting production. Immutability
protects your backups against this scenario, including when Xen Orchestra itself, or the
credentials it holds, have been compromised.
Xen Orchestra supports two approaches:
• On S3-compatible object storage, use Object Lock, configured at the bucket level with
a retention period longer than the retention of your backup jobs. Once written, objects
cannot be deleted nor overwritten before the end of the lock period, even with valid
credentials.
• On your own file server, install the Xen Orchestra immutable backups service (@xen-orchestra/immutable-backups),
running as root on the server itself. It marks backup files immutable at the filesystem
level once they are complete, and lifts it again so that expired backups can be removed.
This service runs independently of Xen Orchestra, so a compromised Xen Orchestra
cannot alter the protected files. Its engine was rewritten in Xen Orchestra 6.3 to re-
duce its memory footprint on large repositories.
The immutable repository must be administered separately from the rest of the infrastruc-
ture: different credentials, no administrative access from Xen Orchestra or from the hy-
pervisor management network, and restricted physical and remote access. Otherwise, an
attacker who takes over your management plane could simply disable immutability. Refer
to the official documentation for setup details8.
Xen Orchestra is not the only option: since summer 2026, Veeam Backup & Replication
officially supports XCP-ng as well. Whatever the tool you choose, the principles described
in this section (isolation, encryption, immutability, monitoring and restoration tests) remain
the same.

A Xen Security Problem Response Process
For convenience, the whole Xen Security Process is integrated in this document. The original
content is available online on the Xen Project website [18].
A.1 Scope
This process primarily covers the Xen Hypervisor Project. Specific information about fea-
tures with security support can be found in SUPPORT.md in the releases’ tar ball and its
xen.git tree and on web pages generated from the SUPPORT.md file. For releases that do
not contain SUPPORT.md, this information can be found on the Release Feature wiki page.
Vulnerabilities reported against other Xen Project teams will be handled on a best effort
basis by the relevant Project Lead together with the Security Response Team.
A.2 Specific Process
We request that anyone who discovers a vulnerability in Xen Project software reports this
by email to security (at) xenproject (dot) org. (This also covers the situation where an ex-
isting published changeset is retrospectively found to be a security fix) Immediately, and
in parallel: those of us on the Hypervisor team who are aware of the problem will notify
security@xenproject if disclosure wasn’t made there already. If the vulnerability is not al-
ready public, security@xenproject will negotiate with discoverer regarding embargo date
and disclosure schedule. See below for detailed discussion.
Furthermore, also in parallel: security@xenproject will check whether the discoverer, or
other people already aware of the problem, have allocated a CVE number. If not, we will
acquire a CVE candidate number ourselves, and make sure that everyone who is aware of
the problem is also aware of the CVE number. If we think other software systems (for exam-
ple, competing hypervisor systems) are likely to be affected by the same vulnerability, we
will try to make those other projects aware of the problem and include them in the advisory
preparation process.
(This may rely on the other project(s) having documented and responsive security contact
points)
We will prepare or check patch(es) which fix the vulnerability. This would ideally include all
relevant backports. Patches will be tightly targeted on fixing the specific security vulner-
ability in the smallest, simplest and most reliable way. Where necessary domain specific
experts within the community will be brought in to help with patch preparation.
We will determine which systems/configurations/versions are vulnerable, and what the im-
pact of the vulnerability is. Depending on the nature of the vulnerability this may involve

sharing information about the vulnerability (in confidence, if the issue is embargoed) with

C Xen Orchestra Security Process
C.1 Context
For the Xen Orchestra (XO) team, ensuring security is not just a priority but a fundamental
mission. Xen Orchestra is built on a NodeJS framework, offering functionalities through both
a Command Line Interface (CLI) and an Application Programming Interface (API). Given
our reliance on npm, a substantial portion of our effort is directed towards ensuring the li-
braries we use are free from security vulnerabilities. This ongoing vigilance extends not only
to third-party libraries but also to our own codebase. It’s important to note that scanning
our public repository for security flaws might not yield fully relevant results, as all non-
production dependencies, such as development dependencies, are not included in the Xen
Orchestra virtual Appliance (XOA) that our users receive.
C.2 Scope
We provide security support exclusively for the "stable" and "latest" release channels of
XOA, and for installations from the sources tracking the master branch. Our policy is not
to extend security support to older or outdated installations. This is because keeping your
Xen Orchestra installation up-to-date is essential for maintaining secure operations. Users
are encouraged to consistently update their systems to the latest version to ensure they are
protected against any known security vulnerabilities.
C.3 Appliance Security
The security of the entire virtual appliance, not just the Xen Orchestra software, is under
continuous scrutiny. At the start of each new release cycle, which occurs monthly, we up-
date all dependencies and conduct a thorough review of any reported security issues to
ensure they are addressed by the time the next release rolls out. Except in very rare cases
or when critical flaws are identified, we do not issue security-only patch releases. Security
fixes land in the "latest" channel first, and in the "stable" channel with the next monthly
release; when a vulnerability is serious, the corresponding advisory provides a mitigation,
and sometimes a verified patch, for appliances on the "stable" channel. The best way to
maintain security in XOA is by using the integrated updater, which automatically keeps your
system up to date and secure without any manual intervention required.
Dependencies are continuously scanned (including with GitHub Dependabot), and unused
dependencies are removed to reduce the supply chain attack surface. Supply chain inci-
dents in the npm ecosystem are assessed and published as advisories even when Xen Or-
chestra is not affected, as was the case for the September 2025 npm attack (VSA-2025-
001).

D Overall Vates security process
At Vates, our commitment to security transcends the products we create. It is a core prin-
ciple that permeates every level of our organization. We understand that maintaining a se-
cure environment is critical, not just within our product offerings but across all aspects of
our company, including our website and any other company-related resources.
To facilitate effective security management, we have established a direct channel for re-

any other Vates-related entity, we strongly encourage reporting these concerns via email
to security at vates dot tech. This open line of communication ensures that all security is-
sues are addressed promptly and effectively, reinforcing our overarching commitment to
protecting both our users and our organizational integrity.
While each project keeps its own security process, the publication of advisories is central-
ized under the Vates Security Advisory (VSA) system, which covers XCP-ng, Xen Orchestra
and XOA, the Vates websites and services, and related supply chain or dependency issues.
Each advisory is identified as VSA-YYYY-NNN and describes the nature of the issue, the
affected products and versions, its severity and impact, and the mitigation and resolution
steps. All advisories are published at https://docs.vates.tech/security-advisories/

(https://docs.vates.tech/vsa/rss.xml); a machine-readable index is also avail-

Each advisory carries two ratings. The upstream CVSS score gives the global severity of
the vulnerability, as assigned by the upstream project. The Vates severity rating (Critical,
Important, Moderate or Low) reflects the actual impact on supported Vates deployments:
a vulnerability with a high CVSS score may be rated Low if the affected component is not
shipped, or if the affected feature is not used by our products. This distinction keeps us
transparent about the global risk, while giving accurate guidance on what you really need
to do.
When a vulnerability is reported to us privately, we follow a coordinated disclosure process:
we coordinate with upstream projects when needed, develop and validate a fix, and then
publish the advisory. We never delay publication for commercial reasons, and security up-
dates are never gated behind a paid support contract.
